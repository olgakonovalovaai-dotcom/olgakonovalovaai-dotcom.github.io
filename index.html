My Approach

From business problems to solutions that make a difference.

I don't start with technology. I start by understanding the problem, finding the right solution, and defining how we'll know it works.

01

Understand the real problem

I talk to stakeholders, explore how things work today, and identify what's slowing people down, creating extra work, or causing problems.

02

Challenge the request

The solution people ask for isn't always what they actually need. I question assumptions, clarify expectations, and look for simpler and better ways to solve the problem.

03

Turn ideas into clear requirements

I translate business needs into clear processes, rules, user stories, and acceptance criteria. I make sure different scenarios and exceptions are covered.

04

Connect the pieces

I identify the systems, data, integrations, and people involved. I look at dependencies and limitations to make sure the solution will work in practice.

05

Design for real-life situations

I plan for missing data, errors, unusual cases, and decisions that need human approval. A reliable solution should work beyond the ideal scenario.

06

Define what success looks like

Before launch, I identify what should improve and how we'll measure it. This could mean less manual work, faster processes, fewer errors, better quality, or lower operational risk.

07

Test, launch, and improve

I work with users to test the solution, resolve issues, support the launch, and use feedback and results to make improvements.

Measuring What Matters

Saving time is good. Creating business value is better.

An automation can save hours without actually saving money. That's why I look beyond how many tasks it completes or how much time it saves.

  1. 01

    How much work does it save?

    I compare manual effort before and after automation, including the time still needed for reviews, corrections, and exceptions.

  2. 02

    What happens to the time saved?

    Saving 100 hours doesn't automatically mean saving 100 hours' worth of salary. I look at whether that time can actually be used for other valuable work.

  3. 03

    Does it deliver better results?

    I consider what changes for the business: faster turnaround, fewer mistakes, more work handled, better service, or lower risk.

  4. 04

    What does a successful result cost?

    I look beyond the cost of running an automation. I consider development, licenses, maintenance, human review, and fixing errors — not just the cost of a single run.

  5. 05

    Is the value real or estimated?

    I separate expected benefits from measured results. Estimates help make decisions, but they shouldn't be presented as proven savings.

My guiding principle

An automation isn't successful just because it runs. It's successful when it solves a real problem and creates value we can actually measure.
